The matter of my graduate recommendation (baoyan) is, by today, finally nearing its end. Looking back on these past months, I can hardly tell whether I was lucky or unlucky. At times my luck felt absurdly good; a few steps further, and fate seemed to love slamming a door half-shut the moment I glimpsed a bit of hope. So the whole way, I swung between "maybe I can" and "maybe I can't," bouncing between hope, disappointment, self-doubt, and picking myself back up. If I had to pick one word for this journey, it would be — a twist of fate. Many things didn't go the way I first imagined; many things I thought I'd lost came back, in another form, from somewhere unexpected.

At the beginning, I was like everyone else preparing for baoyan: revising my résumé again and again, reading professors' homepages over and over, sending out email after email. Back then I believed that if my résumé were a little nicer, my wording a little more careful, my research experience a little clearer, I might get a little closer to the platform I dreamed of. Along the way I was lucky to meet a wonderful senior mentor, and to intern at a higher-level lab. Once inside, I saw for the first time, concretely, that the research environment I had only ever imagined from papers, websites, and social media really did exist. And during that time, I wanted more and more to know: if I kept walking forward, how far could I actually go?

Then "dumb luck" arrived for the first time. The acceptance email from the Shanghai AI Lab summer camp came at a little past 2 a.m. It seems absurd even now — of all days I happened to be awake, and of all times I happened to see it. I was genuinely happy in that moment. What made me even happier was that the professor I had contacted seemed, in my eyes then, truly excellent. Why did I think so? Let's just say I read his social media plenty (laugh). Watching how he interacted with students, the atmosphere of the group, what everyone worked on — I truly believed: this professor is outstanding, kind to students, and probably the kind of person who pushes himself harder than he pushes his students. At the camp I met many brilliant classmates — genuinely strong ones. Though I'd like to think I wasn't too bad either, *knock on wood*. I especially owe my team leader and teammates from those days; we battled through nearly 24 hours together. Looking back, the details of those late nights, discussions, and project sprints are remembered more vividly than the final result. The regret is that I didn't earn the "excellent camper" title, and didn't become that professor's student. It was the first time I felt so close to an opportunity I loved — yet just short of it.

Later, to my surprise, SJTU's summer camp admitted me too. I even wondered whether the dumb luck had struck again — I can't say. I'm grateful to a senior who contacted Professor Cao and a grad student on my behalf to run a mock interview for me. They were both very serious about it, and it gave me a bit more confidence before the real thing. When the actual assessment came, the interview wasn't the grilling I'd imagined; most questions went smoothly — except spoken English. Once I opened my mouth, it was roughly "harsh discord to the ear," to borrow the old phrase — one professor couldn't help asking, "Did you even pass CET-6?" I could only smile awkwardly. The good news: everything else went fine, and in the end, I got the excellent-camper title from SJTU.

At that point I thought the hardest hurdle was over. Later I learned the real difficulty was the mutual matching. I contacted professors round after round; many emails went out, replies were few. Some professors said "let's stay in touch," some never answered, and some seemed hopeful but never gave a clear result. Meanwhile word kept coming that SJTU's PhD slots were being cut, and each bit of news sank my heart a little lower. But despair aside, the work had to go on. So I kept searching, kept reading homepages, kept reading papers, kept looking for my "professor of my dreams."

![Panda astronaut plaster figurine](/images/blog/yinchayangcuo/03-panda-astronaut.jpg)

Eventually the PhD quota was published, and sure enough, far fewer than last year. The day it came out, I contacted again every professor who had said "stay in touch." Some said "the quota isn't out yet, wait a bit," some said "you're within my consideration," and then — nothing. Looking back now, I can't really blame anyone. Admission is a two-way choice; professors have their considerations, students have theirs. But in the middle of it, no one in that position can be so calm. Every "stay in touch" invited a little more hoping; every email that went nowhere whispered, maybe you're still not good enough.

Then one professor gave me an assessment opportunity, in the AI4S direction — read papers and reproduce results. I completed it earnestly, but as I worked, I gradually realized I didn't feel much pull toward that field. Strange, really: when I thought I had almost no options left, facing a possible opportunity made me only more certain — if I took a place just to have a place, in a direction I didn't love, I would regret it. The professor did offer, and I declined. Thinking about it now, that was probably the first time in the whole baoyan process that I truly began learning to take responsibility for my own choices. Not "which school is better," not "which offer is rarer," but a real question: am I willing to spend years of my life in this direction?

From there, I felt my chances at SJTU were nearly gone, so I dramatically widened my search, reaching out to professors at the "mid-tier nine" universities. Alas, most emails sank like stones. So I'm truly grateful to every professor who replied, even when nothing came of it. At that stage, a simple reply could make me feel that at least someone had seen my effort.

Meanwhile I studied each school's training policies, tuition, and admissions, and suddenly discovered that many things aren't as simple as "where do I want to go." Many schools had canceled the academic master's track, leaving only professional master's and direct PhD; and some schools' professional master's tuition was, for me, a crushing burden — squeeze me dry and I still couldn't pay it. Direct PhD meant another kind of risk: five years or more, with uncertainty in research, in direction, in advisor — all to be weighed seriously. Through this whole process, I also questioned my own research potential more than ever before: am I really suited for a PhD? Do I truly love research, or just the idea of "research sounds nice"? If one day there were no papers, no positive feedback, months of failed experiments, would I still want to continue? These questions once felt distant; after baoyan, they all landed on my desk at once.

Add the practical constraints, and the list of schools kept shrinking. Wuhan University didn't admit outside "211" students into its pre-recommendation round; HUST gave almost no academic master's spots to outside students; the bars at Southeast, Sun Yat-sen, Tianjin, and Beihang were high and I mostly didn't clear them; UESTC used referrals and I contacted too late, when many professors had no slots left. Slowly, doors on the map closed one by one. So I turned to the local "big brothers" in Xi'an, and the "one CET, one lake" back home — Central South and Hunan. By then I was almost thinking: fine, since there's no way forward, I'll just go home.

![Afternoon sunlight in the teaching building](/images/blog/yinchayangcuo/09-campus-sunlight.jpg)

Then I contacted a professor at Central South. Academically demanding and full of ambition — actually very close to what I hoped for in an advisor. I took their assessment and earned the excellent-camper title there too. During the assessment I met a Central South student who made a point of asking about this professor; the feedback was the same: excellent, and an excellent person. Later, because a direction I loved even more appeared, I ultimately told the professor I was withdrawing. I remain grateful to him — the further along I went, the more I understood that in the matching process, sincerity itself is a precious thing.

Then I contacted a professor at Xi'an Jiaotong. In our first online chat, his voice was already a bit hoarse — maybe from guiding students all day (laugh). My strongest impression: a "workhorse" who pushes himself relentlessly. And by chance, mid-conversation, we discovered we were both from Hunan — both Hunan kids, both of the "endure hardship, fight stubbornly" spirit. Such moments feel wonderful: two strangers on either side of a screen, suddenly a little closer because of a shared hometown. Later he called specially to say he already had a primary candidate, and asked whether I'd be willing to be the alternate. I gladly accepted, though I knew the odds were slim.

After that, my "long-silent" inbox finally rang again. A professor at Dalian University of Technology asked to chat online, and the conversation was a joy. He was warm, put no pressure on me, and instead kept coaching me — how to revise my slides, how to present my research, which parts best showed my strengths. And throughout, he kept affirming me. Having been through so many rejections, waits, and silences, that affirmation mattered more than I can say. For the first time I felt strongly: professors don't see students only through paper counts, school labels, and tags. Someone was genuinely willing to listen — to what I had done, why I did it, and what I wanted to do next.

Then the DUT path began its own "twist of fate." When the pre-recommendation admission emails went out, I didn't receive one. I thought I hadn't gotten in, that maybe I had lost this professor for good — and then the next day, a supplementary admission email arrived. Fate seems to love this: scare you half to death first, then say "just kidding." Of course, I later learned all of that was only the warm-up act.

The day before my DUT assessment, I first had to submit materials at my own school. From 9 a.m. I was running between buildings and dorms, barely stopping. That afternoon, heading for the airport, I suddenly remembered: DUT's notices were posted in a DingTalk group. I opened it — done for, I had missed the interview time-slot draw. Panic is the only word; the interview was the next day. I posted in the group, sent emails, made calls, and told the DUT professor right away. He kept comforting me, saying not to worry, they'd find a way. But an anxious person can't hear comfort. I kept dialing — from 17:20 to 18:10, twenty-some calls, no answer. That day I could have entered the national finals of the "About to Cry from Anxiety" competition; I truly was about to cry, thinking: after all this preparation, am I really going to crash on "didn't see the group message"? At last the call went through, and the teacher on the line said it was already being handled. The moment I hung up, my whole body finally unclenched; I told the professor, and hurried on to the airport. At the time I thought: this must be my darkest hour. Later I learned — how young I was.

The truly dramatic day came later, on the day of my Northwestern Polytechnical (NPU) assessment. Up at 8:30, in a taxi to NPU, idly scrolling through Beihang Computer Science's camp admission list. Suddenly I saw a name "Chen *-ming" — my heart leapt. Could it be? A closer look at the ID number: not me. Combined with the fact that I'd received no email — confirmed, it wasn't me. I told the Beihang professor I'd contacted; he expressed regret, and mentioned in passing that his own senior schoolmate had just enrolled this year — also that professor's student. In that moment it struck me: he was the "wonderful advisor" another senior had once mentioned to me. A strange vertigo of near-misses rose up — I had once been so close to some very good people, very good opportunities.

The taxi kept rolling toward NPU when Hunan University called — notifying me of my online interview order. Extremely lucky, and extremely unlucky: I was first. I thought it was about to begin and asked the driver, "How much longer?" "About eight minutes." He even sped up — but one cannot exactly begin one's baoyan interview hunched over a laptop in the back seat of a taxi. So I waited the whole ride for the second call, the meeting link. We reached the NPU CS building; I rushed in, found a small sofa and table on the ground floor, set up the laptop, and kept waiting. As I waited, NPU's own assessment drew nearer. I'd heard from the professor there that a policy briefing preceded the assessment; I thought, fine, Hunan hasn't called yet, I'll head up to the fifth floor. Just as I reached the elevator, the laptop suddenly rang out: "Is student Chen XX here?" My entire mind reduced to one word: doomed. I scooped up the laptop, ran back to the table, and began the Hunan University interview in emergency mode. Mercifully it opened with an English self-introduction — the previous pre-recommendation rounds had all been English too, so that part was well-practiced, and the questions afterward stayed within what I'd prepared. Interview done, off I went to the NPU assessment.

At last I met the professor I'd contacted. He was indeed warm, great with students. At noon, he and a few of us students had lunch together and talked a lot. At the table I learned one student's partner also planned to come to NPU — and I felt my stomach drop. That afternoon the professor said he'd discuss intentions one by one, and while I waited, a notice arrived: my thesis defense. My slides — not made. So I sat in the hallway frantically making slides, then was called in. The professor told me: third alternate. He had only one slot, and the first candidate was roughly who I'd guessed. Walking out, I wasn't even that sad — just, well, of course. Still no fate between us.

Back at school I hurried the defense, stumbling through slides and answers. When it ended, I could roughly sense the result wouldn't be great. That night I suddenly remembered Dalian — the panic over the missed time-slot draw, which had felt like my darkest hour. Now I understood: one's definition of "darkest" keeps getting revised. A darker night always makes yesterday's black look less black. And indeed, afterward, apart from my home school, no new pre-recommendation chances came. At that point, all the frantic motion suddenly stopped. This months-long chaos finally began to wind down.

Curiously, in the days after I had already decided, professors from SJTU, USTC, and XJTU reached out one after another. Reading those messages, my feelings were mixed. On one hand — what fortune, that professors still remembered me; on the other, I no longer wavered. After long thought, I chose the DUT professor. The reason wasn't complicated: the direction genuinely interests me, and the professor treats me well. That's it. After months of tug-of-war over "school rank," "advisor fame," "direction," "tuition," "master's or PhD," "research or industry," I came increasingly to feel: whether a choice truly fits you is something no ranking table can answer.

When making choices, we always hope to find the "optimal solution." But most of life has no standard answer. What we ever possess is limited information, limited ability, and a decision that must be made by a certain moment. So I came to accept this: many things cannot wait to be decided until "fully certain." You can only, at that moment, make an earnest choice you're willing to be responsible for, based on everything you know — and then keep walking.

Looking back on the whole season, of course I have regrets. I didn't become the student of the professor I liked from the start; some opportunities missed by a single step; some professors who said "stay in touch" went silent; some camps never admitted me; some good choices had to be abandoned because the direction wasn't right. And many times I couldn't help wondering: what if that email had gone out earlier? What if I'd earned that excellent-camper title? What if I'd prepared better for that assessment? What if I had one more paper from undergrad? The easiest trap in life is these "what-ifs."

But then it struck me: if everything had gone as I originally planned, I would never have arrived here. The things that felt so regrettable, step by step, pushed me onto another road. A "twist of fate" doesn't necessarily mean fate is toying with you. Sometimes it's a reminder that life is never a straight line drawn in advance. We tend to stand at one node and magnify the gains and losses before us infinitely. One camp, one offer, one interview, one unanswered email, one alternate spot — any of them feels like it could decide a life. But stretch the timeline, and they may all be small bends in a very long curve.

Things develop through twists and turns; people too. Sometimes forward, sometimes back; sometimes you feel you've finally neared the life you wanted, and the next day reality kicks you back down. But given a long enough timescale, many unbearable things eventually find their place. So now, I've grown fond of a very plain sentence: do your best, and heed fate. I once thought it a little passive; now I see it is deeply active.

"Do your best" means: before the result appears, do everything within your power as well as you can. Revising the résumé, sending emails, preparing interviews, reading papers, patching projects, rushing slides, making those twenty-some phone calls, clutching a laptop in a taxi, finishing a defense in a hallway — even when hope seemed gone, trying one more time. "Heed fate" means: once everything you can control is done, allowing things not to go as you hoped. Not all earnestness is rewarded at once; not all sincerity is repaid with results; not all effort leads where you first wanted to go. But the effort itself does not therefore lose its meaning.

I used to think the point of effort was to get the result I wanted. Now I think its deeper point is this: when the result arrives — good or bad — you can calmly say, "I did my best." That is probably my biggest change through this time. From frantically hunting for "the best choice," to slowly learning what a "choice that fits me" is; from doubting again and again whether I was good enough, to accepting that I am ordinary while believing I still have room to grow; from fearing I'd miss any opportunity, to understanding that opportunities are many, and life is long. One gain or loss decides nothing.

I've also come to believe in the long view. Seen across a month or two, many things feel like the sky is falling. Didn't get into the camp — sky falling. No email — sky falling. Professor didn't reply — sky falling. Third alternate — sky falling. Defense went poorly — sky falling. But a few days later you look back: the sky was always there, and you were still moving forward. Stretch the scale to three, five, ten years — much of what keeps us tossing tonight may not even be remembered in full detail. What remains may only be: that person, back then, truly lived earnestly, truly fought for things, truly made choices.

And later I realized this isn't only the pain of baoyan students. Everyone around me seemed to be living their own version of chaos. Some doing baoyan, anxious over one slot; some job-hunting, résumés and interviews on repeat; some preparing for the entrance exam, sitting in the library from dawn to dusk; some torn between grad school and work; some worried they'd never find a direction they love; some holding an offer yet wondering if something better exists. Everyone looked busy, and each carried their own pain.

Scrolling social media, it seems everyone else is gliding forward — offers earned, titles won, papers published, jobs found — while you alone thrash in place. But talk with people long enough and the truth surfaces: no one is really that effortless. People post the results, rarely the 2 a.m. anxieties, the unanswered emails, the rounds of self-doubt, the dazing in hallways, the sudden collapses. Everyone has their own stretch of night. So now I don't want to define anyone by a single result — including myself.

I'm grateful to everyone I met along the way. The professors who guided me; the seniors who ran mock interviews; the professors who gave me chances; the professors who replied sincerely even with no slots to offer; the team leader and teammates who fought beside me; the people who told me "it's okay" when I was anxious; and the many who only briefly crossed my path yet offered kindness at just the right moment. Often, what a person truly remembers is not what they finally obtained, but who truly listened when they were most panicked, who gave them one word of affirmation, who reached out a hand. These things may last longer than an offer.

As for myself, I can finally give this stumbling creature a small nod of approval. My luck was indeed good; much of the time I was indeed quite clumsy; my spoken English remains "harsh discord to the ear"; I have no legendary tale. Only this: every time the road seemed to end, I somehow took one more step. Sometimes that's how a road is made — not because you knew the destination from the start, but because there was still one step left, so you took it.

Now, my friends are still searching for schools, preparing for job season, fighting for the entrance exam — while my stretch of turmoil has, for now, come to a close. Fewer emails, less waiting, no more endlessly refreshing notifications. In the sudden quiet there's a faint strangeness, but more than anything, calm. This road had so many twists of fate, and countless times I felt fate was tormenting me. Later I saw: that's probably just what fate is. It never tells you in advance which missed chance will carry you toward another one; which impassable road will force you to know yourself anew; which failure, years later, will make you think "thank goodness it didn't work out then."

So looking back now — the unanswered emails, the nearly-won chances, the moments I nearly cried from panic, the screens still glowing at 2 a.m., the self rushing between taxis, hallways, airports, and classroom buildings — they don't seem so wretched after all. Together they compose this stretch of real life. I still don't know whether today's choice will prove "optimal," or where I'll be in a few years. But at this moment at least, I know why I chose it, and I'm willing to bear everything that follows. That is enough.

Fate handles the twists and turns; we handle living earnestly. Do your best, heed fate; look far, walk near. Most things need not be judged lucky or unlucky the moment they happen — time will explain them for us. What we can do is carry a little sincerity, keep a little persistence, remember the kindness met along the way, and then, through each tug-of-war with fate, slowly learn to make peace with ourselves.

Twists and turns, round and round, arriving at last at calm. And to friends still in baoyan, still job-hunting, still studying for exams, or standing at any other crossroads of life: don't doubt your path just because others have already arrived. Everyone lives in their own time zone. Sometimes not seeing the road doesn't mean there is no road ahead. Walk this stretch well, and leave the rest to time.
